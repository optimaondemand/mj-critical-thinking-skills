📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why the "Fact-Opinion Distinction" is Bogus
Welcome to today's lesson on critical thinking and academic success. We're diving into a big idea: the so-called "fact-opinion distinction" is a myth. That's right — the idea that facts and opinions are always two totally separate things is wrong. Let's break this down together and figure out what's really going on.

By the end of this lesson, you'll not only understand what facts and opinions really are, but also how they overlap, how they differ, and why this matters for thinking critically, making decisions, and succeeding in life.

1
I will be able to explain why a single statement can be both a fact and an opinion.
2
I will be able to distinguish a mere opinion from a substantive opinion.
3
I will be able to say how I would test a substantive opinion against evidence.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT ARE FACTS AND OPINIONS, REALLY?

If you went through the US public education system, you were probably taught: "Facts are things that are true or can be proven" and "opinions are what you believe or feel."

Here's the problem: this distinction between facts and opinions is demonstrably false.

Let me show you.

Facts

A fact is a true statement, that is, it's simply a statement that truly describes the world. Recall that statements can be true (it matches the real world) or false (it doesn't). For shorthand we call true statements "facts."

  • Example of a true statement, i.e. a fact: "George Washington was the first president of the United States."
  • Example of a false statement: "Squares can be circles."

A fact is just a correct statement about reality, about the way the world is.

Opinions

An opinion is a belief you hold about a statement, whether you think the statement is true ("It is my opinion that this statement is a fact").

  • Example of an opinion: "I believe George Washington was the first U.S. president." Said another way: "I believe the statement 'GW was the first US prez' is a fact."
  • Another example of opinion: "I like cheese." Said another way: "I believe the statement 'cheese is tasty' is true."

So, opinions are simply statements of belief.

⚡ THE KEY INSIGHT · FACTS CAN BE OPINIONS!

Did you catch that? I already demonstrated to you that facts and opinions are not necessarily distinct things, that is, that something can simultaneously be a fact and an opinion. Let me show that again:

  • Fact: George Washington was the first president of the U.S.
  • Opinion: I believe that George Washington was the first president of the U.S. (My belief about the statement 'GW was the first US prez' is that it is a fact)

You see: one claim that is both true (a fact) and believed (an opinion). So facts and opinions aren't in conflict. QED.

📚 PART 2 · THE TWO DIMENSIONS OF STATEMENTS

To make sense of this, let's think of facts and opinions as two dimensions of any statement:

1. Truth. Does the statement accurately describe the world?

  • If yes, it's true. We call true statements "facts."
  • If no, it's false.

A statement is simply a description of the world (broadly construed); it is true (or false) depending on whether it correctly describes the world (or not). (If this is helpful: truth is a relationship "out there" between the statement and the world.)

2. Belief. Do you believe the statement accurately describes the world?

  • If yes, it's your opinion that it does.
  • If no, it's your opinion that it does not (or you are not yet decided).

An opinion is simply a belief that the statement correctly describes the world (or not). (Opinions are in your head but about the truth relationship "out there" between the statement and the world.)

🎬 SIMILAR TAKE, ANOTHER ANGLE

To help us understand this better, think about film. We can think about film along at least two lines:

  • Genre: Science fiction, romance, action, etc.
  • Runtime: Short or long.

Would it make sense to say only science fiction can be short, or only romances can be long? Of course not. Film can be any combination of genre and runtime:

  • A long sci-fi: The 1984 version of Dune (the long cut was 6 hours!!).
  • A short sci-fi: They're Made out of Meat (an artsy 9-minute adaptation of the Terry Bisson short story).
  • A long romance: Titanic (nearly 3 hours of strong feelings and questionable choices).
  • A short romance: The Screamers (literally 76 seconds including credits).

Just like genre and runtime can combine in any way, facts and opinions can combine too.

📚 PART 3 · MERE OPINIONS vs. SUBSTANTIVE OPINIONS

Okay, so what about statements like "Brussels sprouts are tastier than carrots"? This doesn't seem to be a description of the world like other statements are. There's no way for this statement to be an accurate description of the world since it's up to each person's preference whether or not they think Brussels sprouts are tastier than carrots. So what gives?

Great insight. This is where things get nuanced. There are some opinions — let's call them mere opinions — that are totally personal and not based on external evidence; that is, not based on anything but your own, personal sense or preference. In other words, these statements are still about the world — since each person is a part of the world — but you only have to look at how well the statement describes your own inner experience to determine whether it is a fact or not.

Compare the above to determining whether your belief that GW was the first US prez is also a fact or not — to figure out if your opinion is true, you have to look out at the historical facts of the world: Does your opinion match the way the world is? If so, your opinion is a fact. But for the statement "I love Brussels sprouts!" the only part of the world you need to look at is your own experiences with them — do you tend to really enjoy them or not?

No amount of evidence will prove or disprove this to anyone else — it's just a statement about your personal preference.

But not all opinions are mere opinions. Some opinions — let's call them substantive opinions — can be supported with evidence. For instance: "Fish are good swimmers." If you hold this, then it is a substantive opinion that is also a fact because there's plenty of evidence to support it (like how fish use fins to propel themselves through water quickly and accurately).

The difference is this:

  • Mere opinions can't be proven right or wrong but by your own experience (e.g., "I like Brussels sprouts").
  • Substantive opinions about the world can be evaluated with reasoning and evidence (e.g., "Fish are better swimmers than jellyfish").
⚠️ PART 4 · WHY THIS MATTERS FOR CRITICAL THINKING

The fact-opinion distinction isn't just wrong — it's dangerous. Why? Because it encourages people to think that opinions can't be challenged or improved.

But here's the truth: opinions can and should be evaluated. You want your opinions to match reality as closely as possible. The more true things you believe and the fewer false things you believe, the better decisions you'll make and the more successful you'll be in life.

For example:

  • If you correctly believe it's a fact that studying regularly improves grades, and you act on that belief, you're more likely to succeed in school.
  • If you mistakenly believe it's a fact that studying doesn't help, you might not work hard and your grades could suffer.

Similarly, if you mistakenly believe that all value statements are mere opinions, all morality (all claims about what's right or wrong) becomes merely a matter of personal preference. To see how dangerous that is, consider:

If it's neither true nor false that murder is wrong (because it's a mere opinion), it's literally okay for anyone to murder anyone so long as the murderer doesn't get caught (because it still might be illegal).

That's a bad result!!

Murder is wrong. Slavery is wrong. Stealing is wrong.

Feeding the poor is good. Helping one's neighbor is right. Sunsets are beautiful. Fish are good swimmers.

And now you know it.

📚 PART 5 · PRACTICAL STEPS FOR THINKING CRITICALLY
  1. Question your beliefs. Ask yourself, "Why do I believe this?" Is there evidence to back it up?
  2. Distinguish substantive opinions from mere opinions. For example, if you believe something about history, science, or math, check the evidence.
  3. Value truth. Remember that the more your opinions align with facts, the better equipped you'll be to navigate the world.
  4. Be open to change. If new evidence comes along that challenges your beliefs, don't ignore it. Being wrong is just part of the process of becoming smarter.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI HAS OPINIONS TOO
When an AI assistant tells you something with confidence, that's a statement. The AI believes the statement (or is trained to act as if it does). Is the statement actually true? That's a separate question. AI confidence is not the same as truth. Always ask: is this a substantive claim with evidence behind it, or is it just the AI sounding sure? Your job is the same as with any other source: check whether the statement matches the world.

💪 THE TAKEAWAY

Remember: facts and opinions aren't enemies. They work together to help us understand the world. Facts are statements about reality, and opinions are what we believe about those facts. By learning to think critically, evaluate evidence, and challenge your own opinions, you'll set yourself up for success — not just in school, but in life.
