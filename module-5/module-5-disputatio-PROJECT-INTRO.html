The Disputatio
🔊 READ ALOUD — LISTEN TO THIS PAGE
A disputatio (Latin for "dispute") is a structured intellectual debate — one of the oldest practices in scholarly tradition. Medieval universities held them publicly: a student would defend a thesis, anticipate counterarguments, and respond to them in real time. You're going to do the same thing, in a VR Disputatio Arena you build yourself. By the end of this project, you'll have produced a researched, fact-checked, defended argument on a substantive topic — and a self-assessment of how you used (and didn't use) AI to get there.
📚 PERMITTED DISPUTATIO TOPICS

Choose one topic from the list below. These have been picked because they're substantive (real arguments exist on both sides) and researchable (real sources exist that you can actually read).

Are video games art, or just entertainment? Should AI be allowed to create music and images? Was the Roman Empire beneficial or harmful to those it conquered? Should fast fashion brands be restricted or banned? Is it more important to explore space or solve problems on Earth first? Do social media platforms cause mental health problems? Was the American Revolution justified? Should we spend resources saving endangered species? Was ancient Greek or Roman civilization more advanced? Should streaming platforms keep content exclusive to theaters? Is it ethical to use animals in medical research? Should college athletes be paid for their play? Does Shakespeare's work deserve to be taught today? Should zoos exist, or do they do more harm than good?

Your teacher may approve an additional topic on request — ask before you start researching.

🔎 HOW TO RESEARCH
  1. Start with popular sources. News articles, magazine pieces, op-eds, podcasts. These help you find the public debate.
  2. Move to scholarly sources via JSTOR. Your teacher will share access details. JSTOR holds peer-reviewed academic articles — this is where you find serious arguments and evidence.
  3. Apply Module 4 skills — preview, predict, ask questions, read the lines and between them, summarize and synthesize, cite everything.
  4. Look for the "great conversation." No serious topic is brand-new. Find the arguments that came before yours. Who has said what? What's still unresolved?
🧠 THE "GREAT CONVERSATION" METHOD (USING AI WELL)

There's no such thing as a brand-new idea. Every position you might take has been argued before. Finding the existing conversation is part of doing real intellectual work. Here's the method — AI can help, but YOU drive:

  1. Read a starter article on your topic. As you read, note every concept, name, event, theory, or term you DON'T fully understand. Make a list.
  2. Take that list to AI. Ask for context on each item. ("Who is X? What is the Y argument? When did Z happen?")
  3. Note the conversations you became aware of. What schools of thought, named debates, key thinkers, or recurring arguments did AI surface for you?
  4. Build a fact-check list. Anything AI told you that feels load-bearing to your argument — write it down. You'll verify it against primary sources (news, JSTOR, books) BEFORE using it.
  5. Self-assess. Which areas of knowledge do you want to build for yourself? Which are you comfortable using AI as a tool for? (You'll answer this in the capstone.)
⚠️ THE RULE
AI tells you about a topic. YOU verify everything that's load-bearing. AI's job is to map the landscape; YOUR job is to walk it and check the ground. If an AI claim ends up in your argument unverified, that's not research — that's gambling on what someone else's autocomplete said.
🎿 THE 3 VR STAGES + CAPSTONE

Each lesson adds one stage to your VR Disputatio Arena. By the end of Lesson 3, the arena is built and you're ready for the capstone.

Stage 1 · Arena Setup + Thesis Wall (Lesson 1: Basics of Argumentation)

Choose your space (Greek agora, modern forum, town hall, seminar room — your choice). Pick your topic. Post a one-sentence thesis. List your starter premises.

Stage 2 · Argument Map + Counterargument Wall (Lesson 2: Evaluating Arguments)

Use the drawing tool to draw your argument's logic. Anticipate the THREE strongest counterarguments and post each on a counterargument wall. Apply the three evaluation strategies from Lesson 2 to find weaknesses in your OWN argument.

Stage 3 · Fact-Check Station + Refinement (Lesson 3: Reviewing and Refining)

Build a fact-check station (a labeled whiteboard) for claims that need verification. Mark each as verified, unverified, or uncertain. Refine your thesis and premises in response to counterarguments and fact-checks.

Capstone · The Disputatio (Course Final Project)

Build the Great Conversation timeline (a timeline marker). Record a defended presentation (a recorded video) of your thesis with counterarguments addressed. Submit a written argument document (Word). Complete the AI self-assessment.
🏆 CAPSTONE DELIVERABLES

At the end of the module — AND the course — you'll submit three things:

  1. Written Argument Document — a Word document with your thesis, supporting premises (with citations), the three strongest counterarguments and your responses, and your fact-check log.
  2. Disputatio Presentation (Recorded Video) — recorded inside your VR arena. Walk through your thesis, your argument map, your counterargument wall, and your fact-check station. Defend your position out loud.
  3. AI Self-Assessment — a short reflection on which knowledge areas YOU built and which you used AI to help with. The "what do I want to become good at? what am I okay depending on AI for?" answer.
