Academic Integrity: Notes, Paraphrasing, and Citing
Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Be a respectful scholar — take notes, rephrase accurately, and give credit.
Congratulations, scholar! You've traveled far. You can preview, ask questions, zoom in and out, understand structure, summarize, and synthesize. But there's one more essential skill that ties it all together — and protects your credibility as a thinker. This lesson is about Academic Integrity: what it means, why it matters, and how to put it into practice through note-taking, paraphrasing, and citing your sources.

1
I will be able to take notes that actually help me think.
2
I will be able to paraphrase honestly — using my own words without copying.
3
I will be able to cite my sources properly in my annotated bibliography.

📚 PART 1 · SMART NOTE-TAKING (THE TWO-COLUMN METHOD)

Three golden rules:

  1. Only write what matters — main ideas, facts, and key terms.
  2. Use your own words — restate things how you understand them.
  3. Mark sources clearly — note the article name, author, or link.
Notes My Thoughts
"More teens are getting less than 7 hours of sleep." (Article: Teen Sleep Crisis)I wonder if school start times affect this. Check other sources.
"Sleep loss can impact memory and focus."Maybe link this to test scores? Useful for paper.
📚 PART 2 · PARAPHRASING — SAY IT IN YOUR OWN WORDS

Paraphrasing means restating someone else's idea in your own words while keeping the original meaning.

Example:

Original: "Sharks have survived for hundreds of millions of years thanks to their highly efficient senses."

Weak paraphrase (too close to the original): "Sharks have lived for millions of years because of their efficient senses."

Strong paraphrase: "Strong senses have made sharks long-time survivors."
✅ The Three-Step Process:
  1. Read the sentence carefully.
  2. Cover it up — don't look.
  3. Say it in your own words like you're explaining it to a friend.

⚡ TRY IT · STRONG, WEAK, OR PLAGIARISM?
The original says: "Coral reefs are home to about a quarter of all marine species." For each rewording, what is it?
"Coral reefs are home to roughly 25% of all marine species."
"About one in four ocean species lives somewhere on a coral reef."
"Coral reefs are home to about a quarter of all marine species."
📚 PART 3 · CITING — GIVE CREDIT WHERE IT'S DUE

A citation tells your reader: "I got this idea or fact from someone else — here's where."

Rule of thumb: If it's not your own idea, observation, or common knowledge — cite it.

Simple citation format (for school assignments):

  • Title of the article, book, or website
  • Author or source name
  • Publication Date (if available)
  • Link (for web sources)

Example: (From: "The Importance of Sharks," National Geographic, 2020)

What doesn't need a citation? Common knowledge. "The Earth orbits the sun." "Water boils at 100°C." "The American Revolution ended in 1783." But specific opinions, data points, or theories — cite them.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND ACADEMIC INTEGRITY
If you use an AI tool to draft text and submit it as your own, that's the same kind of integrity violation as copying from a website without crediting. The work isn't yours; the thinking isn't yours. AI can help you brainstorm or check your understanding, but the writing you turn in needs to be the product of your reading and your reasoning — and any AI-generated text you use should be acknowledged the same way you'd cite any other source.

💪 THE TAKEAWAY

Academic integrity isn't about avoiding trouble. It's about becoming the kind of person people trust — in school, in life, in the world. When you take clear notes, paraphrase honestly, and give credit to others, you're not just being a good student. You're building your reputation as a real thinker.
