📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Learning to argue doesn't mean learning to shout the loudest. It means using logic and evidence to build strong cases for your ideas.
Hey, high-level thinkers! You might think an "argument" is just a loud back-and-forth with your sibling about whose turn it is on the PlayStation. Today we're stepping into a much cooler kind of argument — argumentation, the skill of using logic and reason to convince someone of your point of view. This is the foundation for the Disputatio project.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the parts of an argument — premises and conclusion.
2
I will be able to tell the difference between deductive and inductive arguments.
3
I will be able to build my own argument with clear premises and a clear conclusion.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT IS AN ARGUMENT?

An argument is a collection of statements where some are used to support another. It's like building a case — but instead of "because I said so," you use facts and logic to back it up.

  • Statements are sentences that can be true or false ("truth-apt"). "The earth orbits the sun." "The moon is made of cheese." (Both are statements.) "Give me the PlayStation" is NOT a statement — it's a command. "Runs fast to" isn't even a proper sentence.
  • Premises are the statements that DO the supporting — the reasons or evidence.
  • Conclusion is the statement that gets supported — the main point you're trying to prove.
Example argument:
  1. Premise: All humans need oxygen to live.
  2. Premise: Maria is a human.
  3. Conclusion: Maria needs oxygen to live.
📚 PART 2 · DEDUCTIVE vs. INDUCTIVE

When one statement supports another, it does so in one of two main ways:

Deductive: The premises FULLY support the conclusion. IF the premises are true, the conclusion MUST be true.
P1: All birds have feathers. P2: A penguin is a bird. Conclusion: Penguins have feathers. (Inescapable.)
Inductive: The premises make the conclusion LIKELY, but not guaranteed.
P1: Every penguin I've seen has feathers. P2: This animal looks like a penguin. Conclusion: This animal probably has feathers. (Likely — not certain. Maybe it's sick; maybe it's a 3D-printed penguin; maybe it's not a penguin at all.)
⚡ TRY IT · DEDUCTIVE OR INDUCTIVE?
For each argument, which type of support is happening?
"All squares have four sides. This shape is a square. Therefore, this shape has four sides."
"Every swan I've ever seen is white. So all swans are white."
"I've eaten at this restaurant five times and the food was always great. So the food will be great tonight."
📚 PART 3 · INDICATING TERMS & SPOTTING ARGUMENTS

Certain words signal whether you're looking at a premise or a conclusion:

Premise indicators:
"because," "since," "for the reason that"
Conclusion indicators:
"therefore," "thus," "consequently," "so"

These are signposts on a road, guiding you through the structure of the argument. Watch for them in the curated topics you'll research for the Disputatio.

Heads up — arguments aren't the same as examples or illustrations. "Gotham had 132,000 violent crimes last year" is a stat, not an argument. "Jenny gets good grades because she does her homework on time" is an illustration of one person's habits, not an attempt to convince you to do the same.

📚 PART 4 · BUILDING YOUR OWN ARGUMENT
  1. Determine the topic / conclusion. What are you trying to convince someone of?
  2. Collect evidence. Facts or observations that support your point.
  3. Figure out how the evidence supports the conclusion. Is the link real?
  4. Compile your argument. Put premises and conclusion in clear order.
Premise: Students who study in quiet rooms focus better.
Premise: Focusing better leads to better grades.
Conclusion: Therefore, studying in a quiet room leads to better grades.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND ARGUMENT STRUCTURE
AI-generated text often sounds like an argument but isn't structured like one — lots of confident sentences without clear premises and conclusion. When you read AI output, try to write the argument out as P1, P2, C. If you can't find premises that lead to the conclusion, the AI hasn't actually made an argument — it's made a claim with vibes. You'll spot this everywhere once you start looking.

💪 THE TAKEAWAY

Learning to argue doesn't mean learning to shout the loudest. It means learning how to use logic and evidence to build strong cases for your ideas. By collecting evidence, making sure your premises support your conclusion, and reviewing your argument for weaknesses, you'll be well on your way to mastering the art of persuasion.
