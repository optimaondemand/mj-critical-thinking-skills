📚 PART 1 · MAPPING CREDENCES TO BINARY BELIEFS

We learned in the last lesson that there are two general ways of thinking about beliefs: either binary or on a spectrum.

Binary beliefs

We said last time that binary beliefs are like on-off switches: you either believe something is true or you disbelieve that thing is true (that is, you believe the thing is false). But that's not quite the full picture.

There's actually a middle ground for suspending judgment: "I don't think it's true, I don't think it's false — I'm unsure what to think about this, so I'm suspending my judgment (usually: until I get more evidence)." For simplicity, three positions: believe, disbelieve, or suspend.

Degreed beliefs

On top of binary beliefs, we also learned another way to think about beliefs, namely that they come in degrees, aka "credences," aka "confidence levels." You can express a credence in some statement's being true in terms of probabilities:

  • I am 75% confident that I won't draw a Spade from this thoroughly shuffled standard deck of cards right now.
  • I have a credence of 50% that this fair coin will land heads.
  • There's a 0% chance that in the next 3 seconds I'm going to be recruited by Interpol to help catch notorious jewel thieves.

Degreed beliefs go from really strong belief (up to 100%) down to really strong disbelief (down to 0%).

📚 PART 2 · FINDING YOUR CREDENCE (THE RED-MARBLE METHOD)

Let's try an activity. We're going to use the behavior of betting to track how confident you are in something's being true. (No actual bets are being made here.)

Imagine a bag with red and blue marbles totaling 100 marbles. You don't know how many of each color are inside. I'll give you some statements, and you need to decide how many red marbles it would take for you to be indifferent (equally willing to bet) between the statement being true and drawing a red marble from the bag. This helps you figure out your credence.

Example statement: "A fair coin lands heads."

Would you rather bet on the coin landing heads or pulling a red marble when there are 90 red marbles (and 10 blue)? You'd rather bet on the marble — 90% beats 50%. So we adjust the number of red marbles DOWN.

Try 30 red marbles. Now you'd rather bet on the coin — 50% beats 30%. Adjust UP.

At 50 red marbles, you're indifferent. That's your credence: 50%.

The method, in one paragraph:

Pick a number of red marbles. If you'd rather bet on the marble, adjust the number DOWN. If you'd rather bet on the statement, adjust UP. The number where you're indifferent is your credence.

Pop quiz — the mini-deck

Take the four aces of a standard deck of cards: Spades, Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds. The statement: a random draw from this mini-deck is NOT the Ace of Spades.

How many red marbles? Three out of four cards are non-Spades — 75%. So the answer is 75 red marbles.

What about real-life statements without statistics?

You might be thinking: "Coin flips and card draws have objective probabilities. But how do I figure out my credence about whether my mom will let us order pizza tonight?"

Use your relevant background knowledge. Recall similar nights. Find the patterns. Does she usually say yes on school nights? Was today a hard workday for her? Is there a big family event Saturday that might affect her decision? Then translate the strength of those patterns into a number of red marbles. You're not betting on anything — you're using marbles as a way to capture how confident you actually are.

📚 PART 3 · LINGUISTIC SHORTHAND

While we can get precise about confidence levels, it can be tedious to talk in terms of probability in everyday conversation. So here's a handy way to think about beliefs:

  • Very sure something is true — credence of 80% or more
  • Sure — credence of 60-80%
  • Unsure ("not sure") — credence of 40-60%, evidence isn't strong for or against
  • Sure it's not true — credence of 20-40%, you don't think it's almost certainly false but you have good reason
  • Very sure it's not true — credence of 0-20%, certainly or almost certainly false
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · CALIBRATED AI?
A "well-calibrated" thinker says "I'm 80% sure" only when they're right about 8 out of 10 such claims. Most AI assistants are NOT well-calibrated: they speak with the same "confident voice" whether they're 95% sure or 30% sure. Ask the AI to give you a credence with a reason, and treat its confidence with the same suspicion you'd apply to a friend who never says "I don't know."
