Telling the difference between a taste, an opinion, and a claim that deserves evidence
📖 WHY THIS PART OF THE COURSE MATTERS

Being an intellectual isn't just about sounding smart or making neat plans. It's also about knowing what deserves to be believed and why. In this strand, you'll pick up the tools that help you tell the difference between a private taste ("Chocolate is the best!") and a claim that needs real evidence ("Drinking water helps you think clearly"). You'll learn to spot the moment someone tries to escape responsibility with the phrase "That's just my opinion," and you'll practice backing up your own choices with reasons instead of hunches.

📚 LESSON 7 PREVIEW

Facts vs. Opinions: Busting the Myth

For years many classrooms have repeated the rule, "Facts are proven; opinions are feelings." That rule is too simple to be true. Every statement actually has two dimensions: whether it is true (its truth dimension) and whether someone believes it (its belief dimension). Because of that, a single sentence can be both a fact and an opinion if it is true and sincerely held.

You'll also learn to separate mere opinions — statements about taste such as "I like Brussels sprouts" — from substantive opinions that should bend to evidence, like "Fish are good swimmers." Once you notice that opinions can be right or wrong, you'll see why testing them matters. Study habits, game strategies, even the way you choose friends all improve when you weigh the evidence instead of guessing.

📚 LESSON 8 PREVIEW

Moral Facts: Why "Just Opinion" Isn't Good Enough

If we shrug and label every moral or value claim as only opinion, four big problems pop up: (1) we can't call out cruel people, (2) we can't question harmful cultures, (3) we lose any map for making ourselves better, and (4) decision-making — especially about laws or shared resources — gets stuck. This lesson shows how real-world information about harm, dignity, and human flourishing can serve as evidence for moral judgments. You'll finish with a practical toolkit for disagreeing about values in a way that stays respectful and actually helps everyone grow.

🔗 HOW THE TWO LESSONS FIT TOGETHER

First, Lesson 7 trains you to notice where evidence could exist; then Lesson 8 teaches you how to gather that evidence when the stakes are moral. Clear communication from earlier in the course suddenly carries more weight, because you can now explain why a suggestion is reasonable rather than merely personal. These skills set the stage for the upcoming module, where you will collect and update evidence like a true thinker. In short, placing these lessons here ensures that your plans and team-work are anchored in solid, defensible judgments — the very habit of mind this course is designed to build.
