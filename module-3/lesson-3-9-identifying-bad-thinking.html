📚 PART 1 · WHAT ARE FALLACIES?

Fallacies are errors in reasoning. They happen when the evidence presented doesn't actually support the conclusion being drawn. A fancy Latin phrase — non sequitur — captures it perfectly: "it does not follow." The conclusion doesn't follow logically from the evidence.

📚 PART 2 · FORMAL vs. INFORMAL FALLACIES

Formal fallacies happen when the structure of an argument is flawed — the premises don't logically guarantee the conclusion. This is the world of deductive arguments.

Informal fallacies are trickier. The problem isn't the structure but the evidence itself, or the way it's being used. You can't spot informal fallacies just by looking at the shape of the argument — you have to think through whether the evidence is actually relevant.

There are hundreds of named informal fallacies. Here are three common ones:

Red Herring

Definition: Introducing irrelevant information to distract from the real issue.

"I know I didn't clean up, but have you noticed how messy my sister's room is?"

Why it fails: Whether your sister cleaned has nothing to do with whether YOU did.

False Cause

Definition: Claiming a cause-and-effect relationship where none exists.

"I got an A on my history test because I wore my lucky socks!"

Why it fails: Socks don't affect test scores; what you studied does.

Decomposition

Definition: Assuming what's true for the whole is true for each of its parts.

"The team is unbeatable, so each player must be the best!"

Why it fails: An unbeatable team can still have a weak player; teamwork & strategy carry the rest.

Take heart! The names aren't usually worth memorizing. At their core, all fallacies share one trait: the evidence fails to support the conclusion.

📚 PART 3 · BAYES' RULE AS A FALLACY-BUSTER

Bayes' Rule (Lesson 8) gives you a precise way to show why a fallacious argument fails. The trick is this:

If the evidence is just as likely whether the claim is true OR false, then the evidence is IRRELEVANT — and an argument that leans on irrelevant evidence is fallacious.

The 4-step move:

  1. Spell out the claim clearly. Write the real argument the speaker is making.
  2. Name the evidence. What is being offered as support?
  3. Bayesian analysis. If the claim is TRUE, how likely is this evidence? If the claim is FALSE, how likely is this evidence?
  4. Upshot. If the likelihoods are about equal, the evidence doesn't support the claim. The argument fails.
📚 PART 4 · WORKED EXAMPLES

Example 1 — Red Herring (with Bayesian analysis)

Claim spelled out: It's okay I didn't clean my room because my sister hasn't cleaned hers.

Evidence: My sister's room is messy.

If the claim is TRUE (it's okay you didn't clean): how likely is sister's room being messy? About 40% (she lets things go sometimes).

If the claim is FALSE (it's NOT okay you didn't clean): how likely is sister's room being messy? Also about 40% — same person, same habits.

Upshot: Equally likely either way. Sister's room is irrelevant. Argument fails.

Example 2 — False Cause

Claim: My lucky socks caused my A on the history test.

Evidence: I wore my lucky socks and got an A.

If socks DID cause the A: probability of getting an A while wearing them ≈ 70%.

If socks did NOT cause the A: probability of getting an A while wearing them ≈ 70% (your grade depends on studying, not socks).

Upshot: Same likelihood. No reason to think socks caused the A. Argument fails.

Example 3 — Decomposition

Claim: Because the team is unbeatable, each player must individually be the best.

Evidence: The team has an undefeated record.

If every player is the best: probability of undefeated record ≈ 80%.

If NOT every player is the best: probability of undefeated record ≈ 80% (teamwork, strategy, and a few stars can do it).

Upshot: The team's record doesn't actually support the claim about each individual player. Argument fails.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND FALLACIES
AI assistants can produce fallacious arguments that sound confident and reasonable. They especially trip on false cause ("X happened after Y, so Y caused X"), red herrings (changing the subject when challenged), and decomposition (extending a fact about a group to every individual in it). Use the same 4-step Bayesian audit on any AI-generated argument. The same tool that catches your sister's bad excuse catches a model's bad reasoning.
⚡ TRY IT · IS THE EVIDENCE RELEVANT?
For each argument: is the evidence MORE likely under the claim, or about equally likely either way?
1. Claim: "Studying improves my test scores." Evidence: Studied for 10 hours last week and scored 92 (up from 78).
2. Claim: "Studying improves my test scores." Evidence: It was raining the day I studied.
3. Claim: "Eli is honest." Evidence: Eli has brown hair.
4. Claim: "Eli is honest." Evidence: Eli returned a wallet full of cash she found in the cafeteria.
📚 KEY TAKEAWAYS
  1. Evidence that doesn't make one claim more likely than the alternatives isn't evidence at all.
  2. Bayes' Rule helps us spot fallacies by asking the right questions.
  3. An "explanation thaw" — considering all the possible explanations — lets you avoid tunnel vision and think critically about everything on the table.
