🧠 PART 1 · CRITICAL THINKING AND PROBLEM-SOLVING

Analyzing and Evaluating Information

Critical thinking begins with questioning. When presented with information—whether in a textbook, article, or online—you should ask these and other, similar sorts of questions:

  • Who is the author? Consider the author's credibility and any potential biases.
  • What is the main argument or idea? Identify the central thesis or claim.
  • What evidence supports the argument? Evaluate the quality and relevance of the evidence.
  • Are there counterarguments? Think about opposing viewpoints and how well the author addresses them.
  • What is the purpose of this information? Is it to inform, persuade, entertain, or something else?

Applying Logic and Reasoning

Logic is the backbone of critical thinking. It's about connecting ideas in a way that makes sense and leads to a reasonable conclusion. I recommend taking an actual Symbolic Logic class, a course on Probability, or—worst-case—a Critical Thinking class. In the meantime, to practice critically evaluating content by which someone is trying to convince you to do or believe something:

  • Identify premises and conclusions: In any argument, distinguish the basic premises (supporting statements) from the conclusion. For example, in the following argument the first two lines are premises and they work to support the truth of the last line (the conclusion).
🧮 A SOUND ARGUMENT
PREMISE 1.  Every swan I've seen so far has been white.
PREMISE 2.  The past is a good if imperfect guide to the future.
↓
CONCLUSION.  So, the next swan I see will likely be white.
The premises actually support the conclusion. That's what good reasoning looks like.
  • Check for logical fallacies: Be on the lookout for common errors in reasoning, such as false cause, hasty generalization, or strawman arguments. For example, in the following argument many things are going wrong, logically. We can try to give the logical errors fancy names, but the short version is that the truth of the premises has very little if any bearing on the truth of the conclusion. So this is a bad argument.
⚠️ A BAD ARGUMENT
PREMISE 1.  You say Policy X is good.
PREMISE 2.  But you are a Reprocrat (I'm a Demublican).
↓
CONCLUSION.  So Policy X must be bad.
The premises attack the speaker, not the policy. The conclusion doesn't actually follow.
  • Develop your argument: When you present your ideas, ensure they follow logically from your premises and avoid unsupported leaps in reasoning. Key questions to ask of your reasoning include: if my premises were false, would that affect the truth of my conclusion? Do my true premises support other conclusions? Is there a less-strong version of my conclusion that these premises support?

Reflecting on Learning

Reflection is essential to growth in critical thinking. After engaging with new ideas or solving a problem:

  • Ask yourself what you learned: Consider how your understanding has changed or deepened.
  • Consider your thought process: What strategies worked? What could you improve?
  • Plan for next time: Based on your reflection, how will you approach similar tasks in the future?

🔍 PART 2 · RESEARCH SKILLS

Finding Credible Sources

Not all sources are created equal. When conducting research:

  • Use academic databases: Websites like JSTOR, Google Scholar, or your school's library database provide reliable sources.
  • Evaluate the author's credentials: Look for authors who are experts in the field.
  • Consider the publisher: Academic journals, universities, and reputable organizations typically produce credible content.
📊 SOURCE CREDIBILITY LADDER
For a school research paper, climb this ladder from the top down.
⭐ MOST CREDIBLE
Peer-reviewed journals, books by experts, government data (.gov), university research (.edu)
📈 SOLID
Major news outlets (with editors), expert-authored articles, encyclopedias like Britannica
🤠 USE CAREFULLY
Wikipedia (good for finding sources, not for citing), well-known blogs, general news
⚠️ AVOID
Random TikToks, YouTube comments, anonymous posts, Reddit testimonials — not for citing in school work

Collect Relevant Information

Whether you're making a case or investigating a phenomenon, you're going to collect ideas, facts, and examples from those credible sources. As you read, watch, or interview those sources, put to good use the notetaking skills you learned in the previous lesson. This way you both have everything you need in front of you as you make your case or present your findings, and you'll have it in an organized, easily navigated format.

Properly Citing Information

Citations are crucial in academic work because they give credit to the original authors and allow others to verify your sources. Familiarize yourself with different citation styles (e.g., MLA, APA, Chicago) and use them consistently. Worst-case scenario share the author, title, and page number; or the link to the highlight or the timestamp, if it's digital.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND RESEARCH
AI chatbots can write a research paper for you in seconds. They can also confidently invent fake citations — book titles, author names, page numbers that don't exist. This is called "hallucinating," and it happens often. If you use AI to help you find sources, you must verify each citation actually exists, find the source yourself, and confirm the quote or fact is really in there. AI is a starting point. It is not a substitute for doing the reading.

Writing Your Research Findings

Once you have your sources, you need to organize the information logically:

  • Create an outline: Start with a thesis statement, then arrange your research points in a coherent order.
  • Use digital tools: Tools like Evernote, OneNote, or citation managers like Zotero can help keep your research organized.
  • Group related information: Categorize your findings to support different sections of your paper or presentation.

🎤 PART 3 · PRESENTATION & COMMUNICATION SKILLS

Creating and Delivering Presentations

A great presentation is well-organized, visually appealing, and engaging:

  • Start with an outline: Similar to your research paper, structure your presentation with an introduction, body, and conclusion.
  • Use visuals effectively: Incorporate slides, images, charts, or videos that enhance your message, but don't overcrowd your slides with text.
  • Practice delivery: Rehearse your presentation multiple times. Pay attention to your pacing, tone, and body language.
  • Dress the part: Even if your presentation is just for school, treat it like the formal settings you will find in college and in the business world.

Articulating Ideas Clearly in Writing and Speech

Whether writing or speaking, clarity is key:

  • Use clear and concise language: Avoid jargon or overly complex sentences.
  • Support your points with evidence: Always back up your claims with research or data.
  • Maintain a logical flow: Ensure that each point naturally leads to the next, both in your writing and speech.

Engaging the Audience Effectively

To captivate your audience:

  • Know your audience: Tailor your content and delivery style to their interests and knowledge level.
  • Use storytelling techniques: Weave in anecdotes or examples to make your presentation relatable and memorable.
  • Invite participation: Ask questions or include interactive elements to keep your audience engaged.

💪 THAT'S A WRAP

Today's lesson covered the core skills of critical thinking, research, and communication. These abilities are interlinked and crucial for academic success and beyond. As you continue to develop these skills, remember to:

  • Question and evaluate: Always think critically about the information you encounter.
  • Research thoroughly: Use credible sources and organize your findings effectively.
  • Communicate clearly: Whether writing or speaking, ensure your message is logical and engaging.

That's a wrap! Keep practicing these skills, and you'll be well-prepared for any academic challenge.
