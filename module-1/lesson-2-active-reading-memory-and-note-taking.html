📚 PART 1 · ACTIVE READING STRATEGIES

Active reading is all about making the text come alive in your mind. It's not just about passively reading the words on the page, but engaging with them to understand and retain information better. Here are three key strategies:

1. Annotation and Note-Taking

FORGET THE HIGHLIGHTER! As you read, grab a pen or digital tool to jot down notes in the margins AND/or in a notebook. Underlining or highlighting important phrases is nice to refer to later, but it does not get the info in your head. Instead, scribble summaries, write down questions, and make connections to other concepts you've learned. These annotations will help you engage more deeply with the material and make reviewing a breeze.

2. Summarizing and Paraphrasing

After reading a section, take a moment to summarize the main ideas in your own words. This helps ensure you truly understand the material. Paraphrasing (restating information in your own words) also reinforces learning and makes it easier to remember.

3. Identifying Key Ideas and Details

Not all information in a text is equally important. Focus on identifying the key ideas—these are the main points or arguments the author is making. Supporting details are also important, but your first goal is to see how they connect to the bigger picture. Later you can go back and start attaching those details to the big-picture scaffolding of understanding you've built.

✏️ PART 2 · NOTE-TAKING SKILLS

Effective note-taking isn't just about writing everything down; it's about organizing information in a way that makes sense to you and helps you review it later. Here are three popular note-taking methods:

1. Cornell Method

The Cornell method divides your note page into three sections: a narrow column on the left for cues, a larger right-hand section for notes, and a summary area at the bottom. While your teacher is lecturing or the class is discussing content during class or while you're reading, write your notes in the large right section. Afterward, jot down keywords or questions in the left column, and summarize the main points at the bottom. This structure makes it easy to review your notes and quiz yourself.

💡 PRO TIP
Leave space between each idea so you can go back and elaborate, clarify, or make connections later. Here's a sort-of template you can model your Cornell notes on: CornellNotesTemplate.pdf

2. Outlining

Outlining is a classic method where you organize information hierarchically, using headings and subheadings to show the relationship between ideas. Start with the main topic, followed by subtopics indented beneath it, and further details indented under those. Outlining helps you see the structure of the material and is great for subjects with lots of facts and details to remember. This is less handy during a live class, but super effective while reading. 3. Mind Mapping

If you're a visual learner, mind mapping might be your go-to method. Start by writing the main topic in the center of your page. Then, branch out with subtopics and supporting details, using lines to connect related ideas. Mind maps can help you see the relationships between concepts and keep your notes organized in a visual format. 🧠 PART 3 · MEMORY TECHNIQUES

Memory is a tricky thing—it can be hard to remember everything you read or study. But with the right techniques, you can boost your memory and recall information when you need it most. Here are three powerful strategies:

1. Mnemonics

Mnemonics are memory aids that help you recall information. They can be acronyms, like using "HOMES" to remember the Great Lakes (Huron, Ontario, Michigan, Erie, Superior), or phrases, like "Please Excuse My Dear Aunt Sally" for the order of operations in math (Parentheses, Exponents, Multiplication, Division, Addition, Subtraction). Another trick is to picture vivid and bizarre scenes relating key concepts you want to remember. For example, to remember that 'doler' in Spanish means 'to suffer' in English, you might picture a giant dollar bill doubled over in pain. There are even tricks to remembering numbers and formulae (good for math and science), lists, dates, even names and faces! Check out books by Harry Lorayne for more.

2. Chunking Information

Breaking down large amounts of information into smaller, manageable chunks can make it easier to remember. For example, instead of trying to memorize a long list of words, group them into categories. This method works wonders for phone numbers, vocabulary lists, and more.

3. Spaced Repetition

Spacing out your study sessions over time (instead of cramming) is one of the most effective ways to move information into long-term memory. Review your notes or flashcards at increasing intervals—like one day, three days, and one week after first learning the material. This reinforces the information and helps it stick.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND YOUR MEMORY
Some apps now offer to summarize your readings or generate flashcards using AI. Watch out: when AI does the summarizing for you, you don't get the memory benefit. The active work—summarizing in your own words, retrieving an answer, struggling a little—is what actually moves information into your long-term memory. AI can help you check your work after you've done it. It cannot do the remembering for you.

💪 YOU'VE GOT THIS

Now that you've got a solid grasp of active reading, memory techniques, and note-taking skills, it's time to put them into practice. The next time you sit down to study, try combining these strategies:

  • Start with Active Reading: Annotate and summarize as you go.
  • Take Notes Effectively: Choose the note-taking method that works best for you, whether it's the Cornell method, mind mapping, or outlining.
  • Use Memory Techniques: Create mnemonics, chunk information, and plan your review sessions using spaced repetition.

Remember, these skills take time to develop, so be patient with yourself as you practice. The more you use them, the more natural they'll become—and the better you'll do in your studies. Keep practicing, and you'll be amazed at how much easier learning becomes!
