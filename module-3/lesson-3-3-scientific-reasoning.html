📚 PART 1 · WHAT IS SCIENTIFIC REASONING?

At its core, scientific reasoning is about solving problems using a structured process. It involves four main steps:

  1. Describing the problem: Clearly identify the question or issue you're trying to solve.
  2. Formulating hypotheses: Generate possible explanations for the problem.
  3. Testing the hypotheses: Use experiments, observations, models, or other methods to see which explanation holds up.
  4. Repeat: Keep refining, formulating, and testing as needed.

Let's break down each step and see how they apply to a real-world situation.

📚 STEP 1 · DESCRIBING THE PROBLEM

Before you can find a solution, you need to understand what problem you're dealing with. Imagine trying to fix a broken bike without knowing if the problem is with the chain, the tires, or the brakes. You'd be all over the place. That's why defining the problem is the foundation of scientific reasoning.

Example: Ignaz Semmelweis and the Mysterious Fever

As you read, in the mid-1800s, a Hungarian doctor named Ignaz Semmelweis faced a puzzling problem. Women in one part of his hospital were dying of a fever after giving birth, but no one knew why. Semmelweis knew he couldn't solve this without first clearly understanding the problem: "These women over here are getting sick and dying, but the women over there are not."

📚 STEP 2 · FORMULATING HYPOTHESES

Once you have a clear problem, it's time to brainstorm possible explanations or causes of the problem. This is called formulating hypotheses. A hypothesis is basically an educated guess — an idea that might explain why something happens.

For Semmelweis, formulating hypotheses meant first gathering data — observing the conditions in different hospital wards, comparing outcomes, and searching for patterns. The more data he had relevant to the problem, the more good ideas he could come up with as to what the root cause was. The clearer your understanding of the problem, the better the hypotheses can be, the better chance you have of solving it.

Overcoming Explanation Freeze

This step requires creativity! Often, we get stuck thinking there's only one explanation for something — this is called explanation freeze. But to be a good scientist, you need to think beyond the first explanation that comes to mind. Ask yourself, "What are all the ways this could be happening?"

In daily life, you might think an explanation is good if it's plausible — it seems like it could be true. But in science, we look for explanations that are testable. For example, saying that "bad luck" or "an evil spirit" caused the fever aren't good scientific explanations — they can't be tested. But Semmelweis came up with testable ideas, like "Maybe the women are particularly frightened for some reason which makes them more likely to give up and die, or maybe doctors are carrying something from one patient to another that's causing these women to get sick." These are things he could observe and test.

📚 STEP 3 · TESTING THE HYPOTHESES

Now comes the fun part — testing your ideas to see which one best explains the problem. In science, we do this through experiments, models, simulations, or careful observations. This is where you gather evidence to support or reject your hypothesis.

Semmelweis's Experiment

Semmelweis noticed that doctors moved between autopsies and childbirth without washing their hands. He hypothesized that something on their hands was causing the fever. To test this, he introduced handwashing in chlorinated lime before doctors treated patients. The result? The death rate dropped dramatically. His hypothesis held up under testing.

⚠️ KEY CONCEPTS IN TESTING

But here's the tricky thing about knowledge generally and science in particular: just because a hypothesis seems to work doesn't mean it's definitely true.

Sometimes there's a good explanation that fits all the evidence but we later figure out there's one out there that better fits all this evidence (plus some other evidence too) — for example, Newtonian physics explained the motion of planets and objects well enough until Einstein's theory of relativity came along and accounted for anomalies like the orbit of Mercury and the bending of light near massive objects.

Or maybe there are equally plausible, evidence-fitting hypotheses and there's just no way to tell (at least yet) which one is actually right. For example, the Bohr and Copenhagen interpretations of quantum mechanics accept uncertainty as fundamental, but Einstein famously disagreed, insisting "God does not play dice," believing that a more complete theory would eventually explain what they considered unknowable. For now, one view is as likely to be right as the other, contradictory one.

Beware of Confirmation Bias

Confirmation bias happens when you pay more attention to evidence that supports your favorite hypothesis while ignoring evidence that might contradict it. For example, if Semmelweis had only paid attention to the patients who got better after making one kind of (ultimately irrelevant) change, he would have missed the germ-based explanation that turned out to be right.

To avoid this, scientists perform negative tests. This means looking for evidence that could prove their hypothesis wrong. Why? Because if your idea can withstand attempts to disprove it, it's more likely to be accurate. It's like testing your math answer by checking if it makes sense in the context of the problem — just to make sure you didn't make a mistake.

Failed Predictions — What Do They Mean?

When a test doesn't turn out as expected, it's easy to think that the hypothesis is wrong. But sometimes, a failed prediction means that something else is off, like an assumption you made without even necessarily realizing it. Let's use a mind-bending example:

Hypothesis (H): "The shortest distance between two points is a straight line."

This is a mathematical truth … right? But it turns out when we measured the distance between two points in space we found that it's not what we expected. Our hypothesis was wrong. Did we just break math? How could this be?

No, math is okay. Turns out, there are some assumptions built into our tests that we weren't considering when we made these measurements.

Assumption 1: "This shortest-distance rule holds true in flat, two-dimensional space."
Assumption 2: "The universe is a flat, two-dimensional space."

Once we made these hidden assumptions explicit, we figured out (with Einstein's help) that Assumption 2 is wrong — that the universe is not flat, but curved!

This teaches us that in science, a failed test doesn't always mean the hypothesis is wrong. It might mean that one of the assumptions we made needs to be revisited — what was assumed to be true is in fact its own hypothesis that needs to be tested. Understanding this helps scientists dig deeper and refine their ideas.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI HAS WORLD-CLASS CONFIRMATION BIAS
If you ask an AI assistant "Is my hypothesis right?" it tends to find ways to say yes. It mirrors your framing back at you. To use AI well in scientific reasoning, ask the opposite question: "What evidence would PROVE my hypothesis wrong?" or "Argue the strongest case AGAINST my idea." That's a negative test, AI-style.
📚 BRINGING IT ALL TOGETHER

Scientific reasoning isn't just for scientists in a lab — it's a way of thinking that can help you in everyday life. Let's say you're trying to improve your grades:

  • First, describe the problem: "I am struggling to earn good grades on my math quizzes."
  • Then, formulate hypotheses: "Maybe I'm not studying the right way, or perhaps I need more sleep before tests."
  • Next, test your hypotheses: Try studying in a different way or getting extra rest, and see if your grades improve.
  • Finally, iterate: If those don't do it for you, come up with new hypotheses to put to the test. Don't forget to run some negative tests on your favored theory to make sure you're sure it's right.

By thinking like a scientist, you can tackle challenges more effectively and find solutions that really work.

💪 THE TAKEAWAY

Scientific reasoning teaches us to question, to test, and to keep looking for the best possible explanation. By learning to think like a scientist, you're not just memorizing facts — you're gaining tools to solve problems, big and small. So next time you face a challenge, whether in school or beyond, remember Semmelweis and how his willingness to challenge assumptions saved lives. And who knows? Maybe your curiosity and critical thinking will lead to discoveries of your own.
