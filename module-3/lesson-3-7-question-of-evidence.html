How to Evaluate Evidence to Build Better Beliefs

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How much more likely is this evidence if my theory is true than if it's false?
Have you ever been absolutely sure you were right about something — only to discover you weren't? Maybe you thought you left your keys on the kitchen counter, only to find them in your bag. These moments often happen because we misinterpret the evidence in front of us. This lesson is about a single powerful question that — once you know how to use it — lets you gauge the strength of any piece of evidence.

2
I will be able to place evidence on the strength scale: strongly supports / weakly supports / neutral / undermines / strongly undermines.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT IS EVIDENCE? (QUICK REVIEW)

Evidence is any information that helps you decide whether an explanation or claim is likely to be true or false. For example:

  • You think your favorite soccer team will win their next match because their star player is healthy again. That's evidence supporting your theory.
  • You think you'll do well on your next math test because you studied hard. Again, evidence supporting your theory.

But evidence doesn't prove anything outright. It shifts how confident you should feel about an idea. Evidence operates on a spectrum — strongly support, weakly support, neutral, or even undermine.

📚 PART 2 · THE QUESTION OF EVIDENCE (QoE)

To make sense of evidence, you need to ask one crucial question:

How much more likely is this evidence if my theory is true than if it's false?

The idea: whichever theory better predicts the evidence is the theory that gets confirmed when you see the evidence. The bigger the difference between "how likely if true" and "how likely if false," the stronger the evidence.

📚 PART 3 · THE SAT STUDY EXAMPLE (3 STEPS)

Imagine you're preparing for the SAT (scores 400–1600). You take a practice test and score 1000. You study hard. You take another and score 1100. Theory: Studying improves SAT scores. Evidence: Your 100-point improvement.

Step 1. How likely is this evidence (a 100-point jump) IF studying really helps?
Probably pretty likely. Let's estimate 60%.
Step 2. How likely is the same evidence IF studying does NOT help?
Possible by chance, but not common. Let's estimate 20%.
Step 3. Compare.
60% ÷ 20% = 3. The evidence is 3× more likely if your theory is true than if it's false. That's decent support — not iron-clad, but real.

In real life you won't have exact numbers. Your goal is rough estimates: a lot more likely? A little more? About the same? Less likely?

📊 THE STRENGTH-OF-EVIDENCE SCALE
Strongly Undermines
Much less likely if theory true
Undermines
Less likely if true
Neutral
~Equal either way
Weakly Supports
A bit more likely if true
Strongly Supports
Much more likely if true
SAT examples: 1000→1400 strongly supports; 1000→1100 weakly supports; +10/+20 neutral; 1000→900 undermines; 1000→800 strongly undermines.
📚 PART 4 · LUCY'S HOCKEY JERSEY

Lucy believes her favorite hockey team wins whenever she wears her goalie jersey. She wears it religiously and notices that her team has been winning more often than not. Then, one day, they lose.

Apply the QoE:

  • If the jersey really "causes" wins, the team losing becomes unlikely.
  • If the jersey doesn't affect the outcome, the loss is no surprise.

The team's loss is less likely under Lucy's theory than under the alternative. The evidence undermines her belief. She should decrease her confidence in the jersey's magical powers. (Instead, she clings to her belief. This is irrational thinking.)

📚 PART 5 · FIVE TIPS FOR CLEAR EVIDENCE THINKING
  1. Ask the right question. "How much more likely is this evidence if my theory is true than if it's false?"
  2. Avoid binary thinking. Evidence isn't "consistent" or "inconsistent." It's on a spectrum — how strongly does it support or undermine?
  3. Consider alternative explanations. Lucy's team might be winning because of a better coach, not the jersey.
  4. Be willing to change your mind. Strong evidence against your belief should shift your confidence. You don't have to abandon the theory; just hold it less strongly.
  5. Practice estimating likelihoods. You won't have exact numbers in real life — aim for "much more likely," "a bit more likely," or "about the same."
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND COMPETING EXPLANATIONS
When AI gives you an answer, it usually explains one theory well. The Question of Evidence forces a SECOND step: "How likely would this same evidence be if a DIFFERENT theory were true?" Asking AI "what's the strongest competing explanation?" is one of the most useful prompts you'll ever write.

💪 THE TAKEAWAY

Mastering the Question of Evidence isn't just about acing tests — it's a life skill. It helps you make smarter decisions, avoid being fooled by weak evidence, and stay open-minded enough to revise your opinions when better evidence shows up. The next time you encounter a new piece of evidence, stop and ask: how much more likely is this if my theory is true than if it's false?
