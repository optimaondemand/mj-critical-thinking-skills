📚 PART 1 · SO WHAT IS PREVIEWING?

Previewing means taking a look at the text before you really start reading. It's like scanning the menu before ordering your food — you want to know what's available and what you're in the mood for.

When you preview a text, here's what you're looking for:

  • Title and Subtitle: What does the title tell you? Is it asking a question? Making a bold claim? Naming something specific?
  • Blurbs and Flaps: Book covers and jackets often include short summaries — what do these hint about the focus or message?
  • Table of Contents, Index, Glossary: Scan these to spot key topics and how the book is organized — they're like a map of what matters.
  • Headings and Subheadings: If the text is broken into sections, what do those section titles suggest?
  • Visuals: Are there images, graphs, charts, or bolded words? What clues do they give you about the focus?
  • Length and Format: Is it a long article? A short note? A textbook chapter? A whole novel? This changes how deeply you'll dive and how fast you need to move.
  • Author and Source: Who wrote it? Is it from a textbook, a website, a historical document, or something else?

These are all signals that help you get oriented. It's like walking into a new place and getting your bearings before you explore.

📚 PART 2 · NOW, PREDICTING

Predicting is your next move. Based on your preview, what do you think the text is going to say? What's the author's likely point? What might be the key examples, arguments, or questions?

You don't need to be right — you just need to think. Predicting gets your brain ready to spot important ideas, to confirm or challenge what you expected, and to stay engaged.

📝 WORKED EXAMPLE

Imagine you're about to read a chapter titled "How Virtual Reality Is Changing Education."

  • Previewing: You notice three subheadings — "The Power of Immersive Learning," "VR vs. Traditional Classrooms," and "Challenges of VR in Schools." There's a screenshot of a virtual science lab, a diagram comparing learning outcomes, and a quote from a student in a VR school.
  • Predicting: You might guess the chapter will argue that VR offers unique learning advantages but also has limitations or barriers, especially compared to traditional learning.

Now you're not just reading — you're thinking. You're looking to see if your prediction holds up.

📚 PART 3 · WHY THIS MATTERS

You might be wondering: why not just start reading? Why all this prep?

  • Faster Understanding. Previewing helps your brain build a mental map. You can understand and remember more without re-reading every sentence.
  • Better Focus. Predicting gives you a purpose while reading. You're looking for specific things, so your brain doesn't wander.
  • Stronger Memory. When you've thought about what's coming, it sticks better. You're not just absorbing words — you're making sense of ideas.
  • Useful Everywhere. Works for books, chapters, articles, essays, and even notes. Previewing a friend's study guide? Predict what it'll cover before you read the details.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND PREVIEWING
When AI tools "summarize" an article for you, you're getting the prediction step without the previewing step. That's risky — the summary might be subtly wrong or miss the main point, and you won't know because you never glanced at the source yourself. Real reading skill means previewing FIRST, then deciding whether to read deeply or move on. Don't let any tool skip the previewing step for you.
