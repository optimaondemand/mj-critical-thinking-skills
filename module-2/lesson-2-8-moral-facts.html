📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why Treating All Value Claims as Mere Opinions Leads to Big Problems
Welcome back to another critical thinking adventure! Today, we're tackling a tough but crucial idea: the dangers of treating all normative, evaluative, moral, and aesthetic claims as "mere opinions."

By the end of this lesson, you'll understand what these types of claims are, why they're often mistakenly dismissed as "just opinions," and why this mindset leads to serious problems — both practical and philosophical. Most importantly, you'll learn how to think critically about these claims and how to navigate disagreements about them.

🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why moral and value claims are not just "mere opinions."
2
I will be able to name four serious problems that arise when we treat moral claims as merely subjective.
3
I will be able to evaluate a moral claim using reasoning and evidence rather than dismiss it.
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When someone says "Well, that's just your opinion" about a moral claim, what do you usually do?
📚 THE SETUP · WHAT DO WE MEAN BY "MERE OPINIONS"?

A mere opinion is a personal preference or belief that doesn't have to be backed by reasoning or evidence. Think of statements like:

  • "I prefer chocolate ice cream over vanilla."
  • "I think blue is the best color."

These are deeply personal and can't really be "right" or "wrong" for anyone except the person making the statement.

Now consider evaluative or moral claims, like:

  • "Stealing is wrong."
  • "Bullying is wrong."
  • "The Mona Lisa is a masterpiece."

These are not just about personal feelings. They're making a claim about the world that we can discuss, argue about, and even improve our understanding of by thinking critically. That is, we can say that there really are such things as moral facts — facts about morality, beauty, and value generally. Yet, many people dismiss these as mere opinions, as though they're no different from preferring chocolate over vanilla. This mindset causes big problems.

⚠️ THE FOUR PROBLEMS

Problem #1: You Can't Criticize Immoral People

Imagine someone says, "It's okay for me to steal because I don't think stealing is wrong. That's just my opinion." If morality is "just a matter of opinion," what can you say to someone like this? Not much. You can only respond with your own opinion, and now you're stuck in a standoff: "I think it's wrong" vs. "I think it's fine."

Critical thinking about morality — aka "moral reasoning" — gives us a better way. We can examine why stealing, e.g., undermines trust, harms others, and leads to chaos in society. These aren't just personal feelings — they're evidence that supports the claim "stealing is wrong."

(It's worth noting explicitly that I am not making the claim that something is wrong insofar as it causes harm; I'm just using harm as evidence that something (in this case, stealing) is wrong. What makes stealing wrong might be the harm it causes, or it might be the breaking of some universal rule, or it might be violating some function, feature, or principle that arises for us given the sorts of creatures we are.)

When we treat moral claims as mere opinions, we give up the ability to challenge bad behaviors with evidence, logic, or moral reasoning.

Problem #2: You Can't Criticize Immoral Cultures

Here's a tougher scenario. Suppose someone defends a community that treats cheating outsiders in trade as perfectly normal, by saying, "That's just how their culture works, and we can't judge them."

If all moral claims are mere opinions, this argument seems airtight. After all, how can one culture's opinion about morality be "better" than another's? Relatedly, if we can't say that cheating outsiders is wrong, we can't say a community that stopped that practice is any better than it used to be; nor can we say that there's anything wrong with how we do things now, whatever that might be!

But this approach ignores moral facts. We can evaluate cultural practices by asking questions like:

  • Does this practice harm people?
  • Does it undermine human dignity?
  • Does it violate an important aspect of what it is to be human?

By treating moral claims as more than just opinions, we can make meaningful judgments about cultures — including our own — based on reason and evidence.

Problem #3: You Can't Morally Improve Yourself or Others

If morality is just a matter of opinion, then why bother trying to be a better person? Why try to change someone else's mind about their harmful beliefs or actions?

Imagine someone says, "I used to think lying was bad, but now I think it's fine as long as I benefit." If lying is just a personal preference, there's no reason to challenge them. But if lying undermines trust, damages relationships, harms society, or is just simply bad to do then we can offer real reasons to explain why honesty is better.

Moral improvement requires us to treat moral claims as meaningful, discussable, and — most importantly — correctable.

Problem #4: Practical Decisions Become Impossible

Normative and aesthetic claims also matter in practical ways. Consider these examples:

  • Funding Art: Imagine a school must decide which artists to fund with limited resources. If all art is equally valuable because "beauty is in the eye of the beholder," how can they make a decision?
  • Public Policies: When debating laws or policies, like environmental protections or education reform, normative claims about what's "better" or "fairer" guide these discussions. If we dismiss these claims as mere opinions, how can we decide what's best for society?

Practical decisions demand critical thinking about evaluative ("value") claims. We must weigh evidence, consider consequences, and make reasoned judgments.

📚 THE WAY FORWARD · THINKING CRITICALLY ABOUT NORMATIVE CLAIMS

So, how can we avoid falling into the trap of dismissing everything as "just an opinion"? Here are some steps:

  1. Recognize the Difference Between Mere Opinions and Substantive Opinions:
    • Mere opinions are purely personal and subjective (e.g., "I like chocolate").
    • Substantive opinions make claims about the world that can be supported or undermined by evidence and reasoning (e.g., "Stealing is immoral because it violates principles of justice").
  2. Evaluate Claims with Evidence and Reasoning:
    • Ask: What reasons support this claim?
    • Look for evidence that confirms or disconfirms the claim.
  3. Be Open to Moral and Aesthetic Discussions:
    • Don't dismiss disagreements as pointless. Instead, engage in dialogue to understand and evaluate different perspectives.
  4. Seek Moral and Intellectual Growth:
    • Challenge your own beliefs. Ask: What are the reasons that back my opinions? Are those good reasons?
    • Be willing to change your mind when presented with better arguments.
  5. Respect Disagreements Without Avoiding Them:
    • Disagreeing doesn't mean disrespecting. It means taking others seriously enough to discuss ideas honestly.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND MORAL CLAIMS
AI chatbots are often trained to dodge moral claims. Ask one whether something is morally wrong and you'll frequently get "well, it depends on your values" or "different cultures see it differently." That's the "mere opinion" dodge in machine form. You're now equipped to push back: ask the AI for the reasoning on both sides, the evidence, and the consequences. Then make the moral judgment yourself, with reasons.

💪 THE TAKEAWAY

Treating all moral, aesthetic, and evaluative claims as mere opinions is not just lazy thinking — it's dangerous. It leaves us powerless to challenge injustice, improve ourselves, or make meaningful decisions.

By thinking critically about these claims, we can navigate tough questions, make better choices, and create a fairer and more beautiful world.
