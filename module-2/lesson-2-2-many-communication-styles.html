📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Communication Styles — The Art of Saying Things Clearly
Have you ever been completely lost trying to follow instructions? Maybe a teacher gave directions for a project that made no sense, or a friend tried to explain how to do something and left out important details. Communication isn't just about talking or writing — it's about making sure the other person actually understands what you mean.

📚 PART 1 · WHY DOES IT MATTER HOW WE COMMUNICATE?

The way we explain things matters. If we're too vague, people won't have enough information. If we're too technical, they might get overwhelmed. The key is knowing who we're talking to and adjusting how we explain things so that it makes sense to them.

This is especially important in writing. If you've ever read a set of instructions that felt like a foreign language, or if you've been given directions so basic they didn't actually help, then you already know why clear communication is so important.

📚 PART 2 · TWO OPPOSITE STYLES (AND WHY THEY BOTH FAIL)

Let's look at two different explanations of the same task — turning on Wi-Fi on a phone.

Overly Technical Explanation

To activate the Wi-Fi on your device, navigate to the 'Settings' application, then scroll to 'Network & Internet.' Under 'Wi-Fi settings,' you must enable the toggle switch next to 'Wi-Fi.' Next, select the SSID of your desired network from the available networks list. If the network requires authentication, enter the corresponding WPA2 passphrase when prompted. Ensure that the IP configuration is set to DHCP to allow automatic assignment of network parameters, unless manual configuration is necessary, in which case you will input the designated static IP, subnet mask, default gateway, and DNS server information.

What's the problem? This is way too complicated for someone who doesn't already understand Wi-Fi settings. The writer assumes the reader knows what terms like "SSID," "DHCP," and "subnet mask" mean. Instead of helping, this kind of explanation might make someone more confused.

Overly Vague Explanation

To get your Wi-Fi working, just turn it on. Then find the network you want to use and connect to it. If you need to, put in the password. After that, your device should be good to go. If something doesn't work, you might have to fix a few things in the network options, but don't worry, it's easy.

What's the problem? This is way too basic. It doesn't tell the reader where to find the Wi-Fi settings or what to do if something goes wrong. The phrase "fix a few things in the network options" is useless if someone doesn't already know how to do that. Instead of being helpful, this kind of explanation leaves too much out.

📊 THE GOLDILOCKS ZONE OF COMMUNICATION
TOO TECHNICAL
Jargon, assumed knowledge, overwhelm. Reader gives up.
JUST RIGHT
Simple words. Step-by-step. Helpful troubleshooting. Reader succeeds.
TOO VAGUE
Missing steps, hand-waving phrases. Reader is lost.
📚 PART 3 · FINDING THE BALANCE

Both of these explanations fail in opposite ways in the context of getting a relative newbie (but not utter neophyte) on his way. The first is too complex, assuming too much knowledge. The second is too vague, assuming too little.

A clear, effective explanation sits somewhere in between. It provides enough detail to be useful without drowning the reader in unnecessary complexity. For example:

To turn on Wi-Fi, open 'Settings' on your phone and find 'Wi-Fi' under 'Network & Internet.' Tap the switch to turn it on. Choose your Wi-Fi network from the list and enter the password if needed. If you have trouble connecting, try restarting your phone or checking if the password is correct.

This version:

  • Uses simple, clear words.
  • Gives step-by-step instructions without being overwhelming.
  • Includes helpful troubleshooting advice without assuming too much prior knowledge.
📚 PART 4 · WHY YOU NEED TO ADJUST

Different people need different levels of explanation. Think about how you'd teach someone to tie their shoes. If you're helping a younger child, you might say, "Make two loops, cross them, and pull one through." If you're explaining it to an older friend, you might just say, "Tie your laces in a double knot."

The key is knowing your audience. If you're talking to someone who already knows the basics, you don't need to over-explain. But if they're new to something, you have to break it down clearly.

Every time you explain something — whether in writing, in person, or online — you're making choices about how much detail to include and how formal or informal to be. The best communicators adjust their style depending on who they're talking to.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND AUDIENCE
When you ask an AI assistant to explain something, you can — and should — tell it the audience. "Explain photosynthesis to a 5-year-old" gets a very different answer than "Explain photosynthesis to a biology student." Same fact. Different audience. The skill you're building here works in both directions: clearer for people, clearer for AI.
