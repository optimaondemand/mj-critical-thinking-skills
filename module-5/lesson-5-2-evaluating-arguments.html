Evaluating Arguments

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
If you're going to disagree, you need a solid reason — not just "nope."
Today is a super important skill: evaluating arguments. Just because someone makes an argument doesn't mean you have to agree with it. But if you're going to disagree, you need a real reason. And if their argument is well-supported, you might actually be compelled to believe it — even if you don't want to. So the question becomes: how do you properly evaluate an argument? How do you spot the cracks and take it down?
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the premises and conclusion of an argument I'm reading.
2
I will be able to use three strategies to evaluate (and take down) a weak argument.
3
I will be able to apply the same strategies to MY OWN argument and identify its weak spots.
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
When someone makes an argument you disagree with, what's your usual move?
📚 PART 1 · ARGUMENTS ARE LIKE TABLES

Think of an argument like a table. The premises are the legs holding it up, and the conclusion is the tabletop. If you want to take down the argument (the table), you have two moves: knock out the legs (the premises), OR show that the legs aren't strong enough to hold up the tabletop.

The running example for this lesson:
Premise: When she was a kid, several dogs bit her.
Conclusion: So, all dogs are dangerous.
📚 PART 2 · THREE STRATEGIES TO TAKE DOWN AN ARGUMENT

Strategy 1 · Falsify the Premise (knock out the legs)

Show that one or more premises is wrong. If a premise is false, the whole argument collapses. Example: Maybe she wasn't bitten by several dogs. Maybe it was just one. Maybe she was never bitten at all. Boom — leg knocked out.

Strategy 2 · Show the Support is Bad (wobbly legs)

Even if the premises are true, they might not provide enough support for the conclusion. Example: Even if several dogs bit her, that doesn't mean ALL dogs are dangerous. The conclusion is way too broad for that evidence. At best you get "some dogs are dangerous."

Strategy 3 · Add New Information (expose what's missing)

Agree the premises are true and provide some support — but show that important info is missing. Example: What if she was bothering the dogs — pulling tails, poking them? Then the dogs weren't being dangerous, they were defending themselves. New info, conclusion no longer holds.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH STRATEGY?
Argument: "John got a bad grade on his math test. So, he's just bad at math." Which strategy is each rebuttal using?
"Actually, John got a B+ — that's not a bad grade."
"One bad grade doesn't make someone 'bad at math' — one test is way too narrow."
"He was sick that day and missed two weeks of class beforehand."
📚 PART 3 · APPLYING IT IN REAL LIFE (INCLUDING YOUR OWN ARGUMENT)
  1. Identify the argument. What are the premises? What's the conclusion? People don't always lay these out cleanly — your job is to uncover them.
  2. Check the premises. Are they true? Can you find evidence that contradicts them?
  3. Evaluate the support. Even if premises are true, do they logically lead to the conclusion? Or is the argument stretching too far?
  4. Consider what's missing. Could new information change the conclusion?

Here's the twist for your Disputatio: apply these same four steps to YOUR OWN argument. Be your own toughest critic. Anything you don't catch now, an opponent will catch later.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · ASKING AI TO ATTACK YOUR ARGUMENT
One of the most useful AI moves you can make: paste your argument and ask, "Give me the strongest counterargument someone could make to this." AI is OK at being a sparring partner this way — not as good as a serious human critic, but better than nothing. Use this to stress-test your Disputatio thesis before the capstone. Then verify any new info the AI raises against real sources.

💪 THE TAKEAWAY

Just because someone says something confidently doesn't mean it's true. You have the power to evaluate what they're saying — AND what you're saying. The more you practice, the better you'll get at spotting bad arguments AND building good ones.
