📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Evidence is a scale, not an on-off switch.
Welcome! You've probably heard the word "evidence" before — maybe in crime shows or in science class — but what does it really mean? Most people think of evidence as something that "proves" a claim true or false. But that's not exactly how it works! In this lesson, we're going to explore a deeper understanding of evidence, what it means to "support" or "undermine" a claim, and why evidence doesn't always give us the full picture.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT IS EVIDENCE?

In everyday language, evidence is any information that helps you decide if a belief is true. Think of it like clues in a mystery: a fingerprint might be a clue that someone was at the crime scene. But in critical thinking, evidence isn't just about clues; it's about adjusting how confident you are in a claim or explanation. Evidence helps you decide if something is more likely or less likely to be true.

Let's break this down. Imagine you have a theory: Low-carb diets cause weight loss. Your friend decides to try this diet, and she cuts out bread. After a week, she's five pounds lighter! So, does this prove that the low-carb diet caused her weight loss?

Not necessarily. Yes, her weight loss supports the idea that low-carb diets might help with weight loss, but there could be other explanations too. Maybe she was also sick that week and didn't eat much at all. Or maybe she was more active. Evidence like this makes the diet theory more likely but doesn't make it 100% true. It's just one piece of the puzzle.

📚 PART 2 · EVIDENCE ISN'T ALL-OR-NOTHING

A lot of people think that evidence either proves a claim or disproves it. But evidence isn't (usually) that decisive. Think of it like this: evidence is more like a scale than an on-off switch. Each piece of evidence you gather can tilt the scale a little more in favor of a claim (supporting it) or against it (undermining it). This shift in weight changes your confidence (aka credence) about the claim but rarely makes it a sure thing.

Here's an example. Imagine you're watching a crime show. One detective thinks that the suspect committed a crime, but they later find out that the suspect was across town at the time. Does this prove that the suspect is innocent? No! It might undermine the idea that they committed the crime (because it's hard to be in two places at once), but it doesn't completely rule them out. Maybe they used an accomplice, or maybe they set things up from afar. Evidence like this just makes it less likely they did it — not impossible.

⚖️ THE EVIDENCE SCALE
← UNDERMINES
Evidence that makes the claim less likely than before. Tilts your confidence DOWN.
SUPPORTS →
Evidence that makes the claim more likely than before. Tilts your confidence UP.
Most evidence doesn't prove anything — it tilts the scale.
📚 PART 3 · HOW DO WE DECIDE IF SOMETHING IS EVIDENCE?
  • Evidence supports a claim if it makes that claim more likely than you thought it was before.
  • Evidence undermines a claim if it makes that claim less likely than you thought it was before.

Let's go back to the low-carb diet example. If your friend loses weight after cutting carbs, that outcome supports the claim that low-carb diets can help with weight loss. It fits with what the theory suggests might happen, so it makes the theory more likely for you than it was before learning about her 5-pound weight loss.

In math terms: where E is evidence and H is the relevant hypothesis, evidence E supports H if Pr(H|E) > Pr(H) — and undermines H if Pr(H|E) < Pr(H). Don't worry if that's complicated; the main takeaway is that evidence adjusts your confidence up or down.

📚 PART 4 · EVIDENCE IN EVERYDAY LIFE

Let's think about how you might apply this. Say you're trying to decide if a particular study method works well for you. You've read that taking notes by hand helps with memory, so you give it a try for a week and then test yourself on the material. You do well on the test, which supports the claim that taking notes by hand improves your memory, but it doesn't completely prove it.

Why not? Because other factors might have influenced your test results. Maybe you studied extra hard this time, or maybe you already understood the material well. So, while your good test result supports the idea that handwritten notes help with memory, it's only one piece of evidence. To be more confident, you might want to try the same method over a longer period or in different subjects to see if the pattern holds up.

📚 PART 5 · EXCEPTIONS: WHEN EVIDENCE CAN PROVE OR DISPROVE

Most of the time, evidence can't give us 100% certainty. But there are rare cases where it can. For mathematical claims, a single counterexample can disconfirm the claim entirely:

Claim: For any integer n, n is always greater than n+1.
Evidence against: n=1. Since 1 is NOT greater than 2, this evidence proves the claim is false.

This kind of evidence is rare in everyday life, but it shows that sometimes evidence can definitively settle a question. When we're dealing with complex things — like science, people's actions, or real-life situations — evidence usually just makes a claim more or less likely.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI'S "ONE EXAMPLE PROVES IT" PROBLEM
When an AI assistant cites "a study" supporting an answer, that's usually one piece of evidence — not proof. Ask: "What's the strongest evidence AGAINST this claim?" Real evidence-based thinking weighs both directions, not just the side that supports the conclusion you (or the AI) started with.

💪 THE TAKEAWAY

Evidence is like a flashlight that helps you see things more clearly, but it doesn't always give you the full picture. Most evidence supports or undermines — it tilts the scale — rather than proving with certainty. Knowing this is what separates careful thinkers from confident-but-wrong thinkers.
