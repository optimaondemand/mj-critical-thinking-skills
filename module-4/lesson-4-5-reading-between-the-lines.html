📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What's on the surface isn't always the whole picture.
Last lesson, you learned to resolve ambiguity — figuring out what something actually means. Today, we go further: what isn't being said? What does the author assume you already know? What's being implied? What might be hidden?
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify what a text assumes the reader already knows.
2
I will be able to spot what's being implied without being stated.
3
I will be able to notice what an author might be hiding or has forgotten to mention.
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A friend says, "The captain was not drunk today." What's your first reaction?
📚 PART 1 · THE CASE OF SOCRATES (WHAT'S ASSUMED)
Argument: "All mortals eventually die. So, Socrates will one day be dead."

Seems simple, right? It's just saying that Socrates, like everyone else, will die. But look closer: the sentence assumes Socrates is a mortal. The author doesn't spell it out, because they assume you already know Socrates is a mortal human.

Texts are full of these hidden assumptions, and being aware of them helps you see the full picture. Without knowing Socrates is mortal, the conclusion doesn't follow. (Socrates could be a divine being, a fictional character, or an inanimate object — and then he wouldn't be subject to death.)

📚 PART 2 · LETTER OF RECOMMENDATION (WHAT'S IMPLIED)

Imagine you're on the admissions committee for a top-tier graduate program in astrophysics. You receive a letter of recommendation for a student. It says:

"Student X has wonderful penmanship."

Okay, that's nice… but is that really the most important thing for a future astrophysicist? By focusing on the student's handwriting, the letter writer is implying that there isn't much else to say. The letter isn't just saying "X has good penmanship." It's indirectly saying "X is not a great fit for astrophysics." Reading between the lines is how you catch that.

📚 PART 3 · WHY DON'T AUTHORS SPELL EVERYTHING OUT?
  • Saving Time. Many things are common knowledge; explaining all of them is tedious. The author expects you to fill in the blanks.
  • Mistakes. The author thinks something is common knowledge but it actually isn't — either because it's not true, or because you don't happen to know it.
  • Intentional Misleading. Some people want to hide things. If they spell out all the details, you might disagree or catch on to a hidden agenda.
⚡ TRY IT · ASSUMED, IMPLIED, OR HIDDEN?
For each statement, what's the most interesting "between-the-lines" move happening?
"Since everyone loves pizza, no one will complain if we serve it at this event."
"John hasn't responded to my text for an hour. He must be ignoring me."
"There is no lawsuit between that man and our client."
📚 PART 4 · PRACTICAL STEPS
  1. Ask questions. "What's not being said?" "What does the author assume I know?"
  2. Look for implications. "What else follows from this?" Like the astrophysics letter — what's being suggested without being stated?
  3. Challenge assumptions. Just because something is assumed doesn't mean it's true. Ask: "Is this really the case? Does this hold up from another perspective?"
  4. Practice, practice, practice. The more you read critically, the better you get at it.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND HIDDEN ASSUMPTIONS
AI tools bring HUGE numbers of hidden assumptions into every answer — based on what's in their training data, what's popular online, and what's missing. When an AI gives you an answer, ask: "What assumptions is this answer making? Whose perspective is missing?" The same between-the-lines skill that catches a sneaky letter of recommendation also catches the sneaky biases baked into an AI's response.
