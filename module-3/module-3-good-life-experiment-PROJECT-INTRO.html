Module 3 Project
The Good Life Experiment 🧬
Run a real experiment on yourself using the reasoning tools you'll learn this module.
📖 THE BIG IDEA

Living the good life takes effort and purpose. As you learn about trial and error, data analysis, and scientific reasoning in this module, you'll test out these tools of reasoning in a real-life experiment on yourself.

You'll choose one good-life habit or routine, design an experiment to see what actually happens when you do it, collect data on yourself, analyze it, and present what you found — just like real scientists do. Across nine lessons, you'll build up your experiment in a VR research lab. The final deliverable is a video walking us through your lab, presenting your work in the same six-section format used in real scientific papers.

🎯 STEP 1 · PICK YOUR EXPERIMENT (THIS WEEK)

Choose one social experiment to run on yourself for the duration of the module. Each experiment below comes with a specific research question that will guide your work. Pick something you don't already do consistently — the experiment only works if there's actually a change to study.

🧠 Personal Habits & Self-Awareness

The Gratitude Experiment
Write down three things you are grateful for each day for one week. Does practicing gratitude change mood, stress level, or outlook?
Sleep and Focus Experiment
Track sleep hours and attention levels in class for one week. Does more sleep correlate with better concentration?
The Quiet Reflection Test
Spend 10 minutes each day in quiet reflection, journaling, or prayer. Does quiet reflection improve emotional calm or decision-making?

📱 Technology & Social Media

Phone-Free Hour
Avoid phones or social media for one hour each evening for a week. Does unplugging improve focus, family interaction, or sleep?
Positive vs Negative Media Diet
Compare how you feel after consuming uplifting vs. negative online content. Does the tone of media affect your mood?

🧥 Civic Virtue & Community Behavior

The Kindness Ripple Experiment
Perform three small acts of kindness each day (holding doors, helping peers). Do acts of kindness influence the behavior of others?

🌳 Nature & Environment

The Outdoor Time Experiment
Spend 20 minutes outside daily for a week. Does time outdoors affect stress, mood, or energy?

🤝 Service & Responsibility

The Clean Space Test
Organize your workspace for a week. Does a tidy environment improve productivity?

👀 Social Awareness & Empathy

Listening Experiment
Practice active listening (no interruptions, eye contact). Does better listening improve conversations?
Perspective-Taking Exercise
Intentionally try to understand someone else's viewpoint in a disagreement. Does perspective-taking reduce conflict?
✨ Or propose your own
Have a different good-life habit in mind? Clear it with your teacher and design a research question for it before you start.
🧬 YOUR VR RESEARCH LAB

In one of the ENGAGE Locations (a laboratory, study, observatory, even a treehouse — your choice), you'll build a persistent research lab. Each lesson's VR activity adds one component:

Lesson 1 · Hypothesis Hub — your top hypothesis plus three alternatives
Lesson 2 · Scientific Predecessors — real-world researchers who failed at this kind of thinking and what they missed
Lesson 3 · Methodology Wall — what you'll do, what you'll measure, how long for
Lesson 4 · Confidence Scale — your starting belief about what will happen
Lesson 5 · Data Collection Station — daily log starts here
Lesson 6 · Evidence Library — what counts as a positive result, a negative one, an ambiguous one
Lesson 7 · Mid-Experiment Audit — strong-vs-weak evidence wall from your own logs
Lesson 8 · Belief-Update Timeline — how your confidence shifted as data came in
Lesson 9 · Fallacy Audit — review your own reasoning for bad thinking; fix it
Capstone · Research Presentation — final Video MyRec tour of the whole lab in research-paper format
📝 THE COMPANION DATA LOG (OUTSIDE VR)

Alongside your VR lab, keep a daily data log in a composition notebook or an Excel spreadsheet. This is where you actually record what happened each day of the experiment.

For each day, record:

  • Date.
  • Did you do the habit? Yes / No / partial.
  • One specific thing you measured (sleep duration, mood 1–10, focus during school, etc. — you'll decide this in Lesson 3).
  • A one-sentence note on anything notable.

This log is the raw data your Evidence Library (Lesson 6) and Mid-Experiment Audit (Lesson 7) draw from. You'll bring it into VR via screenshots or photos.

📚 YOUR FINAL VIDEO — RESEARCH PAPER STRUCTURE

When you record the capstone Video MyRec walking through your lab, follow the six sections used in real scientific papers and presentations. Each section in your video corresponds to a region of the lab you've built across the module.

I. Problem or Question. What were you testing, and why does it matter?
II. Background. What did you (or anyone) already know about this habit before the experiment?
III. Research Methodology. What did you do, what did you measure, for how long?
IV. Results. What actually happened? Show the data from your log.
V. Analysis. What do the results mean? Did your starting belief hold? How did it shift?
VI. Conclusion. Would you keep this habit? What would you change about the experiment? What's next?
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI ON THIS PROJECT
If you have access to an AI assistant, it can be useful at specific stages: brainstorming hypotheses (Lesson 1), spotting patterns in your data (Lessons 7–8), suggesting fallacies to check for (Lesson 9). It is not useful as the experimenter, the data collector, or the judge of what happened to you. Your experiment is about you; only you have the data. Use AI as a brainstorm partner. Make the decisions yourself.
✅ WHAT A SUCCESSFUL PROJECT LOOKS LIKE
  • A topic you actually tried (even imperfectly), with a daily data log to back it up.
  • A VR lab built up across the module with all nine components in place.
  • A Capstone Video MyRec that walks the viewer through the lab and follows the six-section research format.
  • Honesty about what didn't work or what surprised you — that matters more than "success."

A good experiment that didn't confirm your starting belief is still a good experiment. Scientists call this "a useful null result" — you learned something true.
