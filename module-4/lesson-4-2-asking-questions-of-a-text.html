📚 PART 1 · WHY ASK QUESTIONS?

Imagine reading a mystery novel without ever wondering who did it. Or reading a science article without wondering why this experiment matters. Kind of pointless, right?

Asking questions is what makes reading meaningful. It's what transforms you from a passive reader into an active learner. It's how you go from "just reading words" to actually getting something from them.

Questions help you:

  • Stay focused while reading.
  • Think critically about what the author is saying.
  • Connect ideas to other things you know or wonder about.
  • Remember more because your brain has a purpose.
📚 PART 2 · WHERE DO GOOD QUESTIONS COME FROM? (FOUR TYPES)

Strong readers ask questions before, during, AND after reading. Here are the four reliable sources good questions come from:

1. From Previewing & Predicting

Turn the signals you saw in your preview into questions. Title says "Will AI Replace Teachers?" → "Is this a real concern or a dramatic headline?"

2. From Context

Questions from outside the text: why the teacher assigned it, what unit you're in, what's happening in the world. "Why is this such a big deal right now?"

3. From Your Own Curiosity

The "wait, what?" moments. "Could students cheat with this?" "How would I feel if a robot graded my work?"

4. About the Author's Moves

Not just what they said but how and why. "What tone is this? Is the author trying to persuade me? Do I trust this?"
⚡ TRY IT · CLASSIFY THE QUESTION
For each reader question, which of the four types is it?
1. "Why did the author end with a quote instead of a fact?"
2. "The title says 'Hidden Costs' — what costs is it going to reveal?"
3. "Wait — how does this connect to what I learned in science last year?"
4. "Why is my teacher having us read this article during our climate unit?"
📚 PART 3 · THE QUESTION WEB (A WORKED EXAMPLE)

As you read, imagine you're building a web of questions that surround your understanding. Some strands come from previewing, some from context, some from curiosity, some from analysis. Here's how that looks with a short passage:

"As schools adapt to rapidly advancing technology, many educators are exploring how generative AI can support student learning. Some argue that AI tools can personalize lessons, assist with grading, and provide instant feedback. Others worry these tools might reduce human connection or encourage shortcuts."

Build the web:

  • Preview/Prediction: How will this text describe AI's role in classrooms? Will it take a clear side?
  • Context: Why are people so focused on AI right now? What has changed recently?
  • Curiosity: Would I want to learn from an AI tutor? Better or worse than a teacher?
  • Author's Moves: Why did the author include both positive and negative views? Are they neutral or pushing me to a conclusion?
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · ASKING QUESTIONS OF AI
The four question types work just as well on AI-generated content as on human-written texts. When an AI hands you an answer, ask: What did its training data probably look like? Why did it phrase it this way? Whose perspective is missing? The same questioning move that protects you from a manipulative author also protects you from an over-confident AI.

"Easy or Hard" Dilemmas and Grade Inflation Arguments: Extra credit options are typically either easy or hard. If they are hard then only the best students will complete them, and they already have high grades, but students who "need" extra credit, because they are not learning the regular material and thus have poor grades, will not receive those points. Easy extra credit contributes to "grade inflation," i.e., grades higher than warranted by the students' understanding: a B-earning student in a very hard class might receive an A because of fluff extra credit. And a student with a D-level of understanding shouldn't get a B or A by doing a lot of easy extra credit. Extra credit can contribute to grades not accurately reflecting students' understanding of the course material.
